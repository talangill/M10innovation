Regenerative Transition

Business Integration 1

Learning goals — what to take away from the knowledge clips
Clip 1 — Intro to regenerative business Clip 2 — Earth systems & planetary boundaries Clip 4 — Nature, biodiversity & SBTN Clip 6 — 5 steps to regenerative transition
1
Explain the difference between degenerative, sustainable, and regenerative business using the Bill Reed spectrum — and give a real hospitality example of each.
Clip 1Look for: the spectrum from "business as usual" → traditional sustainability → restorative → regenerative. The key distinction is limiting damage vs. actively healing.
Test yourself
Where on the spectrum does your case hotel sit right now? Where does your concept aim to take it?
2
Name and explain the 4 principles of regenerative business — healing not limiting, systems thinking, place-based & collaborative, and mindset shift — and apply at least one concretely to your case hotel.
Clip 1These are the four markers you use to judge whether an action is truly regenerative. Sustainability initiatives that don't meet these are "just" sustainable — not regenerative.
Test yourself
Pick one principle. What would it look like applied specifically to your hotel's location and client challenge?
3
Describe what planetary boundaries are, identify which of the 9 are most directly relevant to your case hotel, and explain the concrete business risk of crossing them.
Clip 2Focus on the hospitality examples — ski resorts, coral reefs, Baltic Sea algae blooms. Every boundary has a direct industry consequence. Your hotel is exposed to some of these right now.
Test yourself
Which 2–3 planetary boundaries pose the biggest risk to your specific case hotel — and how? Could you explain the financial impact to a hotel owner?
4
Explain what science-based targets for nature (SBTN) are, why nature targets are location-dependent unlike climate targets, and what a land or freshwater target could mean for a hotel in practice.
Clip 4The Maas River phosphorus calculation is a worked example — understand the logic, not just the numbers. The key insight: targets depend on the condition of the local ecosystem, not a global average.
Test yourself
If your case hotel is in the Netherlands, what land target might apply? What would it mean concretely — a green roof, supplier standards, landscape engagement?
5
Walk through the 5-step regenerative transition framework (Get inspired → Seek advice → Check your chain → Set targets → Collaborate) and make at least one specific, well-researched recommendation per step for your case hotel.
Clip 6Note step zero: avoid and reduce harm first. You cannot skip to regeneration. For each of the 5 steps, the clip names specific tools — Conservation Evidence, regeneration.org, restore.eco — use them.
Test yourself
For "Check your chain" — pick one product your case hotel uses and trace a pressure it creates on nature or society. Can you recommend a concrete alternative or supplier action?

Technology

Business Integration 2

Learning goals — what to take away from the reading sources
ScienceDirect — AI & design thinking: synergies & ethics Hechinger Report — students offloading critical thinking to AI CUNY — combining design thinking & generative AI in the classroom Medium — AI-enhanced design thinking workshops
1
Identify which phases of design thinking AI genuinely accelerates — data synthesis, divergent ideation, rapid prototyping — and which phases require human judgment to remain meaningful, then apply this distinction to your own project process.
ScienceDirectCUNYLook for the framework mapping AI tools to each design thinking phase. The key question is not "can AI help here?" but "does AI help produce better thinking or just a better-looking output?"
Test yourself
In your project so far: which tasks did you use AI for? Did it help you think more deeply, or did it produce something you didn't fully understand or own?
2
Explain what "metacognitive laziness" means in the context of AI use — and honestly recognise whether it's happening in your own project work.
HechingerThe key finding: students who used AI improved their essays the most — but learned the least. The AI group spent less time reading, less time evaluating their own work, and more time just interacting with the chatbot. Better output, less learning.
Test yourself
Think of one time this module where you used AI. Did you engage with the result critically — or did you accept and use it without fully interrogating it? What did you actually learn from that interaction?
3
Critically compare human-generated vs. AI-generated ideas and explain why peer and user insights tend to be more contextually specific and emotionally resonant — and what that means for a hospitality concept that must work for real people.
CUNYIn the classroom pilot, students rated AI ideas higher on feasibility and viability — but human ideas consistently scored higher on desirability. One student noted: "AI is helpful for the feasibility and viability options, but the desirability score comes from the actual people."
Test yourself
Look at your current concept idea. How much of it came from AI, how much from your own user research and group discussion? Would a real user recognise their own needs in it?
4
Name and explain at least three ethical and environmental risks of using AI — hallucination, data privacy, algorithmic bias, carbon footprint — and describe what responsible AI use looks like in your specific project context.
CUNYScienceDirectTraining a single large language model can emit as much CO₂ as five cars over their lifetimes. Students in the CUNY pilot also raised concerns about uploading proprietary ideas to open AI platforms. These are not abstract problems — they apply to how you use AI right now.
Test yourself
Have you verified any facts or statistics that an AI gave you in this module? What would happen to your business case if an AI-generated "insight" turned out to be fabricated?
5
Describe how AI is currently being applied in hospitality operations — scheduling, personalisation, guest interaction, revenue management — and assess whether and how your concept could incorporate AI as a specific solution to a specific problem, not as a buzzword.
ScienceDirectFocus on the distinction between mechanical AI (automating repetitive tasks), thinking AI (processing complex data), and feeling AI (social, emotional, communicative tasks). Your concept will sit in one of these — which one, and does that match what your user actually needs?
Test yourself
If your concept includes AI: could you remove it and still solve the problem? If yes, is AI actually necessary — or is it decoration? If your concept doesn't include AI: is there a specific operational problem where it genuinely would help?

Multiple Value Creation (MVC)

Business Integration 3

Learning goals — what to take away from the workshop & tools
Workshop: Finance & MVC (90 min · Concert at Sea case) Format Financial Feasibility 2.0 (Excel template) Scenario Analysis Example (Excel) Schoenmaker & Schramade — Corporate Finance for Long-Term Value, Ch. 1–3
1
Explain what Multiple Value Creation actually means — financial, social, and environmental value created simultaneously, not traded off against each other — and how this differs from traditional CSR or sustainability reporting.
Workshop Q1–2Ch. 1–3The book distinguishes Finance 1.0 (shareholder value only), 2.0 (stakeholder value added), and 3.0 (integrated financial + social + environmental value). Your concept should aim for 3.0 — understand what that actually requires.
Test yourself
Can you explain MVC without using the words "sustainable" or "impact"? If you rely on those words, you probably haven't defined it concretely enough yet.
2
For your case hotel, map the main inputs, core activities, outputs and outcomes — and classify each as financial, social, or ecological value. This is the structural backbone of the MVC model.
Workshop Q3, Q6, Q9The workshop uses the Concert at Sea festival as a practice case. Work through Q3, Q6 and Q9 for that case first — then apply the same thinking to your hotel. The key discipline: be specific about what the output is, not what it's supposed to achieve.
Test yourself
Pick one output from your hotel concept. Is it financial, social, or ecological — or does it create more than one type of value at once? How do you know? What evidence supports that claim?
3
Conduct a stakeholder impact map with double materiality — map key stakeholders by power and interest, define short and long-term goals, and identify positive and negative impacts both ways: how the world affects the hotel, and how the hotel affects the world.
Workshop Q8Single materiality (1.0) asks "how does the world affect me?" Double materiality (2.0) adds "what is my effect on the world?" Your business case needs both directions. The stakeholder impact map is the tool that makes this visible — coaches will look for it.
Test yourself
Who are the two stakeholders most affected by your concept — positively and negatively? For each, can you name both a short-term and long-term goal, and the specific impact your concept has on them?
4
Define KSF-KPI-PI structures across all three value dimensions — not just financial KPIs, but measurable indicators for social and ecological value creation too.
Workshop Q10The tip in the workshop: use stakeholder goals from your impact map as the source for KPIs — if a stakeholder cares about it, it should be measured. A concept without social or ecological KPIs isn't demonstrating MVC, it's just claiming it.
Test yourself
Name one KPI for each value dimension in your concept. Is each one actually measurable with the data your hotel could realistically collect? Or are they aspirational but unmeasurable?
5
Build and interpret the financial feasibility model — complete the "with concept" vs. "no concept" P&L sheets, calculate the incremental impact, and run low/mid/high scenarios to demonstrate the financial dimension of your MVC claim.
Feasibility ExcelScenario ExcelThe feasibility template has paired sheets: "no concept" (baseline) and "with concept" (your proposal). The Incrementals sheet calculates the delta automatically. The scenario analysis example shows how changing one assumption (e.g. occupancy rate) flows through to GOP — replicate this logic for your own concept assumptions.
Test yourself
What is the single biggest assumption your financial model relies on? If that assumption is wrong by 20%, does your concept still make financial sense? Have you tested this in your scenario analysis?

Change Management

Business Integration 5

Learning goals — what to take away from the literature & assignments
Evans — Strategic Management for Tourism, Hospitality & Events, Ch. 13 Robbins — Organizational Behaviour, Ch. 13 Assignments 1–7 → your implementation plan
1
Diagnose what your concept actually requires the organisation to change — classify the type of change (incremental vs. transformational, planned vs. emergent), map every tangible and intangible thing that must shift, and assess the operational implications.
Assignments 1 & 2Evans Ch. 13The type of change determines the timeline and whether the hotel can stay open during implementation. A concept that requires a cultural shift cannot be rolled out in two weeks — and if you claim it can, the change management expert at Pitch 4 will push back hard.
Test yourself
Is your concept incremental (improving something that already exists) or transformational (requiring people to think and work fundamentally differently)? Be honest — most students underestimate how much needs to change.
2
Build a multi-perspective implementation target using the balanced scorecard — define what success looks like across financial, customer, internal process, and innovation/learning perspectives, so every action can be evaluated against a clear standard.
Assignment 2Evans Ch. 13Without this, you can't evaluate whether your implementation is working. The balanced scorecard forces you to define success before you start — not just "the concept is implemented" but what specific outcomes look like from four different perspectives.
Test yourself
Write one sentence per scorecard perspective describing what success looks like for your concept. If you can't write the customer perspective sentence without it sounding vague, you haven't defined your concept clearly enough yet.
3
Build a stakeholder power map and predict resistance — identify who is most affected, what phases of change they will go through, and design specific actions that help them through each phase rather than just announcing the plan.
Assignment 4Robbins Ch. 13Resistance is not irrational — people resist change for legitimate reasons. Robbins covers the sources of resistance and how to address each. A power map without a resistance analysis is incomplete: knowing who is affected is not the same as knowing who will push back and why.
Test yourself
Name the one person or group in the hotel most likely to resist your concept. What specifically are they afraid of losing — status, routine, job security, something else? What would you do in the first two weeks of implementation to address that?
4
Analyse the hotel's organisational culture and design behavioural interventions tied to specific cultural quadrants — not generic training, but targeted actions that shift the specific behaviours your concept needs to succeed.
Assignment 6Robbins Ch. 13This builds directly on your M9 culture analysis. The Competing Values Framework gives you four quadrants — each requires different types of intervention. An intervention that works in a clan culture (collaborative, people-focused) will fail in a market culture (competitive, results-focused). Know which quadrant your hotel sits in.
Test yourself
What is one specific behaviour that needs to increase and one that needs to decrease for your concept to work? For each, what concrete intervention would create that shift — and why does it fit the hotel's culture?
5
Write a credible, phased implementation plan with a realistic timeline, KPIs per scorecard perspective, risk analysis using the Vision-Skills-Incentives-Resources-Action Plan framework, named change agents with justified roles, and evaluation checkpoints at each phase.
Assignments 3, 5 & 7The VSIRA framework is a diagnostic tool: if any one of the five elements is missing, the change fails in a predictable way. Missing vision → confusion. Missing skills → anxiety. Missing incentives → slow progress. Missing resources → frustration. Missing action plan → false starts. Check your plan against all five before Pitch 4.
Test yourself
Run your plan through VSIRA: do you have all five elements covered? Which is weakest? And: who specifically is your change agent — someone already in the hotel, or an external hire? What qualities make them the right choice for this particular change?

Client Presentation (Group + Individual)

5 min pitch + 5 min Q&A · Week 9 · Smart casual / business attire

5-minute pitch structure — choose your style

Classic consulting structure. Lead with the problem, land with the answer. Works well when the client already feels the pain.

  1. Hook (30 sec) — one sharp question or fact that makes the problem undeniable
  2. The problem (45 sec) — strategic issue, who it affects, why now
  3. Your concept (90 sec) — what it is, why it's the right answer, one key visual
  4. How it works (45 sec) — implementation in brief: who, what changes, Service Blueprint
  5. The value (30 sec) — environmental, social, financial impact + 3-year headline figure

Total: 30+45+90+45+30 = 4 min + 60 sec buffer for transitions & breathing room ✓

Narrative structure. Take the client on a journey from pain to transformation. Works well when you want to create emotional buy-in.

  1. Open in the world (30 sec) — paint the current situation through a real user or moment
  2. The tension (45 sec) — what's broken, at stake, or being lost
  3. The turn (90 sec) — your concept as the solution, backed by research
  4. The new world (45 sec) — what the hotel looks like after: guests, staff, financials
  5. The ask (30 sec) — what you're recommending the client does next

Total: 30+45+90+45+30 = 4 min + 60 sec buffer for transitions & breathing room ✓

Research-forward structure. Lead with what you discovered, then show where it led you. Works well when your empathize phase surfaced something surprising.

  1. The insight (45 sec) — one unexpected finding from your research that reframes the challenge
  2. Why it matters (30 sec) — the strategic implication for this hotel, right now
  3. The concept (90 sec) — what you designed in response, and why this solution follows logically
  4. Proof it works (45 sec) — prototype testing results + financial headline
  5. Next steps (30 sec) — implementation roadmap and first action

Total: 45+30+90+45+30 = 4 min + 60 sec buffer for transitions & breathing room ✓
